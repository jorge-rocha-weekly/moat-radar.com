<h1 class="title">Understanding AI from the Inside</h1>
<p class="subtitle">What lies between the prompt we type and the answer we receive?</p>
<p class="byline">BY JORGE ROCHA</p>
</header>
<section class="summary body-text">
<div class="label-mono">Summary</div>
<p>Behind artificial intelligence models lies a complex chain of solutions and suppliers that, at some levels, looks more like an industrial production chain than the digital technology we see on the surface. It spans applications, models, cloud, data centers, software, chips, memory, networks, packaging, manufacturing, equipment, power and cooling.</p>
<p>Looking at the full chain, two characteristics stand out. The first is strong concentration at several points: the three largest cloud providers account for about 61% of the market, three manufacturers for virtually all HBM memory, TSMC for more than 70% of the global foundry market, and ASML for virtually 100% of the EUV lithography used in the most advanced chips. The second is interdependence across layers: having the best model or the most powerful GPU is not enough if memory, connectivity, power or cooling capacity are missing.</p>
<p>This helps explain why bottlenecks shift as infrastructure grows. A constraint may start in chips, move to memory, packaging or networks and, ultimately, reach power availability. At the same time, the largest players are trying to reduce these dependencies: cloud providers are developing their own chips, model companies are moving into applications and agents, and infrastructure manufacturers are expanding into other parts of the chain.</p>
<p>The map also brings back three central questions for Moat Radar: is the current level of Capex building necessary capacity or creating potential oversupply? Where will the next bottleneck be? And, in an application layer that is still taking shape, who will own the customer relationship?</p>
</section>
<main class="body-text">
<p>In recent days, a sharp correction in technology stocks has increased concern about the scale of investment in artificial intelligence and its effects on cash flow and returns on capital across the sector.<a class="footnote" href="#ref-1">[1]</a></p>
<p>That concern makes sense. But it coexists with another equally important reality: AI models are getting better, more widely used and more useful.</p>
<p>Companies already use them to write code, serve customers, create content, research, analyze information and automate tasks. At the same time, a new generation of products, agents and services is being built on top of these models.</p>
<p>That is precisely why it is worth asking a simple question before debating whether we are looking at necessary capex or excessive investment:</p>
<p class="standalone-question">What lies behind the models we are using more and more?</p>
<h2 class="headline">From the screen to the infrastructure</h2>
<p>For users, artificial intelligence is ChatGPT, Claude, Gemini, Copilot, Cursor or another application that incorporates AI.</p>
<p>These applications and agents are the most visible layer of a much larger chain.</p>
<p>Below them are foundation models, such as LLMs (Large Language Models), capable of generating text and code and, in multimodal architectures, also working with images, audio and other formats.</p>
<p>Then come the large cloud providers, or hyperscalers, companies that operate computing infrastructure at enormous scale and provide it as a service.</p>
<p>From that point on, the chain becomes much more physical.</p>
<p>Data centers house thousands of servers. Between infrastructure and hardware there is also an important software layer, with platforms such as CUDA and PyTorch helping developers use and coordinate these computing resources.</p>
<p>Next come GPUs (Graphics Processing Units, processors originally developed for graphics and now fundamental to AI), TPUs (Tensor Processing Units, specialized accelerators developed by Google) and other chips that perform the calculations.</p>
<p>HBM (High Bandwidth Memory) must feed data to these processors at enormous speed. Networks connect thousands of accelerators so they can operate as a single system.</p>
<p>Further down the chain are advanced packaging, which integrates chips and memory at high density; foundries, specialized factories that manufacture chips designed by others; the equipment required to produce those chips; and EDA (Electronic Design Automation) tools used to design and validate circuits.</p>
<p>All of this requires power and cooling.</p>
<p>AI looks like software on the surface. Underneath, it is also an enormous industrial chain.</p>

<p>The infographic below organizes this chain from the user to silicon.</p>
<p>Beyond showing examples of the main companies present in each layer, it tries to answer two simple questions: what does each layer do, and how concentrated is that market or submarket?</p>
<p>The “Understanding the layer” column summarizes the role of each link. The concentration column highlights something that is not always visible when we look only at the best-known names in AI: in several important parts of the system, a small number of companies control a large share of available capacity.</p>
<p>The map also shows some moves toward vertical integration as companies try to reduce dependencies, control costs or get closer to the customer.</p>

<h2 class="headline">A chain with few alternatives</h2>
<p>When we look at the complete stack, meaning the set of technological layers that make up the system, the concentration is striking.</p>
<p>In HBM, three manufacturers account for virtually the entire market. In chip manufacturing, or foundry, a single company accounts for more than 70% of the global market. In EUV (Extreme Ultraviolet) lithography, used to produce the most advanced chips, there is essentially a single supplier.<a class="footnote" href="#ref-2">[2]</a></p>
<p>This concentration did not emerge by accident.</p>
<p>Capital, intellectual property, scale, accumulated know-how, long development cycles and high switching costs have created barriers that take years, and in some cases decades, to reproduce.</p>
<p>That is why, at several points in the chain, there are very few real alternatives.</p>
<h2 class="headline">The whole system matters</h2>
<p>For much of the current AI race, attention has focused on GPUs.</p>
<p>But a more powerful GPU does not solve the problem on its own.</p>
<p>It also needs fast memory, connectivity, packaging, software, power and cooling. As clusters, groups of thousands of computers working in coordination, grow larger, the efficiency of the whole system becomes as important as the performance of any individual component.</p>
<p class="short-emphasis">The relevant unit is no longer just the chip. It becomes the system.</p>
<p>And that helps explain another phenomenon: bottlenecks move.</p>
<p>At one point, the constraint may be accelerators. Then memory or packaging. With larger clusters, networks and interconnects become more important. With denser data centers, transformers, electrical infrastructure, power availability and cooling begin to limit expansion.</p>
<p>Solving one constraint often just reveals the next one.</p>
<p>In the end, a chain that starts with extremely sophisticated algorithms comes to depend on something very basic:</p>
<p class="standalone-question">electricity.</p>
